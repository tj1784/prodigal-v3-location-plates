## 1. The story in one breath

A son who can't bear living under his father's rules asks for his share while his father is alive, so that he can finally have a will of his own. Away from the rules, he loses everything they had quietly kept for him: bread, rest, a lit gate, a brother, a name. The friends he bought fill his place with someone new. He comes home to the father who never stopped keeping them. His elder brother, twenty years older, grew up in the lean years as his father's only help and has kept every rule since, as a servant. He watched the younger one favoured all his life, and now he has to decide whether to come in. Jesus tells all of this to a publican who is weeping and to two Pharisees standing outside a wall.

## 2. The engine: three rules that keep him alive

Asher feels each of these rules as control. Each is shown at home and kept by Eliab without force. Each is missing in the far country, and each pays off.

**The line (Scene 3, the brothers, before supper).** Asher tells Amos first. Amos tells him what the words mean in their time, then: "Thou wishest him dead. Say that I lie. Brother. Say it." Asher doesn't deny it. He answers:

He never says "dead". His silence is the admission (your 5 Oct rule: "I wish u were dead, without words").

## 2a. The two brothers: the lean years, the favour, and how the love dies

Luke builds the ending on one word. Amos says **"this thy son"**, and the father answers **"this thy brother"**. The film earns that word: a real bond at the start, twenty years of the younger one favoured, and a love that dies on screen while Asher is away. By the end Amos is what the Pharisees are to Hezron: the brother who has disowned one of his own.

**Ages.** Eliab about 65, Amos about 40, Asher about 20, the son of his father's old age (Gen 37:3). They never grew up together. Amos was already a grown man working the fields when Asher was born.

**The lean years.** When Amos was a boy there was nothing: no hired men, no servants, just a father and his firstborn on the floor before the sun. A boy's hands were needed, so Eliab pushed him. By the time Asher came, the terraces were bought, the store was full and there were servants to wake instead. Their mother died when Asher was small: Amos had her through the hard years, and Asher had the easy years without her. Three lines carry it, with no flashback:
1. **Micah, Scene 2,** watching Amos thresh beside the hired men as one of them: "I remember when it was thy father and that boy alone on this floor, before the sun. No hired men then."
2. **Amos to Asher, Scene 3:** "When I was thy age I had threshed ten summers. Thou hast slept through all of thine."
3. **Scene 8, the gate at dusk (moved from Scene 5 on 20 Oct):** AMOS: "Thou never didst let me sleep." ELIAB: "When thou wert small, there was no one else to wake. Then there was." And the wound under it: AMOS: "I asked thee once. For a kid, for my friends. I was ten." ELIAB: "We had four goats, and a winter coming." AMOS: "I never asked again." A fair no in a hungry year, carried for thirty years. It makes "thou never gavest me a kid" literally true, and "all that I have is thine" the answer to a ten-year-old.

Before there were servants, Amos *was* the servant, and he never stopped. That is the literal truth of "Lo, these many years do I serve thee", and why the father answers "*Son*, thou art ever with me."

**The father's favour: grace, never injustice.** Eliab is God in the parable, so he never takes anything from Amos. Everything he gives Asher is grace to the son who needs it most. Through Amos's eyes it looks like favouritism, and each moment lands on his face:
- **The purple-hemmed tunic,** a gift from his father, which Asher wears even out with the flock while Amos works in undyed wool. It is the coat of many colours (Gen 37:3). Kallias sees it in the city: "Purple at the hem, like a lord's son. A lord's son never counts." The best robe answers it at the end.
- **He lets Asher sleep** while Amos is on the floor before the light.
- **The lost ewe.** Asher loses her; Eliab goes into the dark, finds her, and puts her into *Asher's* arms: "She is thine to keep."
- **He laughs at Asher's jokes at supper** while Amos eats in silence.
- **He stands beside Asher at the village gate** and makes Nahum weigh true.
- **"Keep a calf on the barley. Always one."** Amos knows exactly whom it is for, and he never got a kid.
- **The robe, the ring and the calf.**

This is the Bible's own pattern: God favours the younger (Abel, Jacob, Joseph, Ephraim, David). Amos even says it without knowing: "these many years do I *serve* thee" is Genesis 25:23, "the elder shall serve the younger". But Jesus breaks the old pattern's cruelty. Ishmael and Esau were sent away; this father goes out to the elder too.

**The love, and how it dies.**
- **Asher's side stays warm:** he teases Amos, looks up to him, and asks him to come: "Come with me. Ask for thine." Warmth that only goes one way.
- **Amos's side is old protectiveness thinning into duty.** When Asher was a small boy, Amos plaited him a shepherd's sling and taught him to use it. Asher still wears it. Amos's lie to cover for him, "I sent the dog out with them", is said through his teeth, for his father's sake.
- **The last kindness is the bend in the road (Scene 6b):** his own cloak, "Father would want thee warm", a short rough embrace, and a joke from Asher that dies.
- **It dies in Scene 8.** The traveller from the village fire comes back to the gate with a rumour: Eliab's son spends like a lord in the cities, with women. That is where Amos's "devoured thy living with harlots" comes from; it is the rumour's version, since Jes is a married woman Asher never paid. Amos watches his father light the lamp night after night for a boy who wished him dead, and disowns him aloud. Micah: "Forty-five. Thy brother's flock is whole." Amos: "It is not his flock. He sold his part." At the lamp: "Thy son is not coming back. And if he does, he comes for more." And: "It is written, the elder shall serve the younger." (His "I have served" line was cut on 20 Oct; the four goats carries it.) From here on he never says "brother".

**The ending.** The servant says "Thy brother is come" (Luke 15:27). Amos says "this thy son". The father says "this thy brother", calling back the bond the audience remembers and Amos has buried. In the doorway Asher holds out the sling his brother made him. Amos looks at it; his jaw sets; he doesn't move. Cut. The person who once protected him most is the one who can't forgive him, which is exactly the Pharisees' position toward Hezron, their brother in Abraham.

## 2b. The fall: thrown out, then unseen (Scene 9)

1. **The last coin.** At the last feast Asher tips his purse for the wine, and a single coin falls out. Across the room, Kallias sees it, writes one more line on his tablet, and draws a stroke under the column. After that night, nobody from the company comes for him.
2. **The rent.** The landlord, speaking to him as "you": "No silver, no room. It is let from tonight. The bags stay, for what you owe." He is put into the street with only what he is wearing. This is Luke 15:14's "when he had spent all".
3. **The famine.** Then the mighty famine. He sells Amos's cloak, then the sandals, the belt, the purse. The merchant points at the sling: "That too." "No." "Keep it, then. You cannot eat it." He sleeps in doorways.
4. **The company.** Night. Lamps come down the street. He hears Philon yell "Eutychos!", and his head comes up: for a heartbeat he thinks he's being called. It's for a new rich boy in a garland, with Kallias at his ear and Jes on his arm, the company on its way to a feast while the city starves. Asher pulls back into the dark and covers his face with his rag, ashamed. They pass within arm's reach and don't see him. He watches them for a few moments as their lamps go down the street, until the dark comes back.

No speeches. The only line in the street is Philon's yell: Asher's own name, now someone else's.

## 2c. The world: bigger than a stage

v2 looked like a stage partly because its own pages emptied it: 65 shots open "Only Asher and Eliab are in this shot" and 258 lines say "Only the people and things the picture shows". Those were H3 safeguards that leaked into the writing. **In v3 the world goes into the prompt text and the location references, and every window describes all the life it reaches.** There are no first frames: on Ref2VA the cast and location references supply what the camera reaches. Keep to your 4 Oct lesson anyway: describe only what the window reaches, never things beyond it. Those "only…" phrases never appear in the v3 screenplay.

**The geography (a top-down map comes before any prompt is written).** The farm sits on a terrace above a valley. The village is below, with its well and the gate where the elders sit. The road east winds down past other farms toward Jericho, and the far hills sit in haze beyond. From Eliab's gate you can see a mile of that road, which Luke 15:20 needs: "when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him."

**Every description has three layers:** something close, the action, and the world beyond (the valley, other farms' smoke, travellers on the road). People are cut by the frame edge.

## 2d. Look, camera and assets

Grok's light, palette and texture, with your camera rules and the Revenant decisions in place of Grok's camera.

**Frame.** 2.39:1, 2048×864 video (locked 29 Sep). Delivered as Rec.709.

**Light (Grok's, corrected).**
- Available light only, from one source. Outdoors, the sun through dust and haze, at each scene's real hour (Scene 1: a low sun through haze). Indoors, a doorway, a window, a clay lamp or the hearth.
- No fill, reflector, beauty light or glamour rim. The shadow side of a face falls dark under the brow, beard and chin (Grok's Caravaggio reference is right).
- Warmth is restrained. The feast is warmer than the far country, but never golden. The famine is a hard, pale, dry light.

**Palette and texture.**
- One sun-faded earth family: dusty linen, ochre, wool brown, olive-grey, chalk, weathered skin. The one rich colour at home is the purple hem on Asher's tunic.
- A pale, hazy sky, never a saturated blue. The city of Fortune gets a little more colour from torches and dyed cloth, still inside the gate below.
- Lived-in: worn wool, dust, chaff, crumbs, motivated sweat, ungroomed hair and beards, cracked lips, dirt in the creases.
- An imperfect camera: fine grain, soft edges, slight motion blur, haze veiling the distance.

**Measured on every location asset and every render** (`look_natural.py`, `clean_meter.py`): brightness 0.28–0.36, real shadow ≥ 18%, colourfulness ≤ 24, no blue sky, sharpness ≤ 750, grain ≥ 1.0.

**Lens.** Spherical ultra-wide, about 14 mm, with deep focus: a very close face and a readable world in the same frame (your 17 Oct decision). Prompts describe it in words, not lens numbers, because image models ignore those: "the face very close and big in frame, the people and hillside behind it still sharp". ## 2e. The cold open and the road home (20 Oct)

Your experiment, written in on 20 Oct: "we need it violent to capture the attention of viewers".

- **The cold open (Sc 1A).** Right after "A certain man had two sons", a starving young man we don't know steals bread in a town on the road west, is run down, beaten, and stoned almost to death by the townsmen. He is left in the ditch under a tamarisk: the fly, the dragging breath, the white sky. Through his eyes, a man steps in, black against the sun and faceless: "Asher… Asher…" Black. Out of the black comes his father's "Asher." at the farm, and he wakes young and whole. The audience spends the whole film knowing where this boy ends up.
- **How violent.** Brutal, but it never lingers on the wounds: the kick, the bread ground into the dust, the heel on his hand, "Here. Eat this.", the brow opened, the boy who throws and looks proud, "They go on throwing for a while after that." The cruelty is in the people, not in close-ups of injuries. That keeps it watchable, and away from YouTube's graphic-violence age gate.
- **Who is in the sun.** Never said. It is God, by your call; the audience may think it is a stranger or his father. My suggestion for H3: give the VOICE Eliab's voice reference, softened. In the parable the father is God, so the voice is true either way, and it cuts straight into Eliab's "Asher." at the waking.
- **Opening B, the farm (A/B test, 20 Oct).** The owner's curve ball: "try to make it look like this is the family", then "only make it look like decent… fool the audience to have that moment to breath before the violence", "dont make them jewish, make them foreigners". Straight after "A certain man had two sons": a serene harvest morning on a rich, green farm on the road west, a foreign household of the Ten Cities (a little clay god in a niche by the door). Servants at the well and the oven; a father, a mother and two sons at a table under a vine. The father gives the first bread to the clay god and the second to his younger son, his hand on the boy's head; the mother teases the elder. The audience takes them for the parable's family and gets a breath before the violence. Then the maid screams "Thief! Thief!" and a ragged stranger runs out of their pantry with a loaf. They catch him in the last standing barley by the wall. Beyond the wall the land is brown and dead: a famine, and the farm the only green thing in it. The elder son tosses the bread to the dog: "You would steal from my father's house?" The hired men hesitate, it is only bread, and he makes his own law: "The law says a thief pays it back. But this is a famine, and in a famine I say the thief dies!" (a counterfeit of Jesus's "but I say"). Stones from their own wall; the favoured younger son hesitates, then throws; the parents watch from the gate and never come down. Afterwards the maid pours water over the men's hands at the well, as before a meal, and it runs red; they sit back down and the father lifts the bread. ## 3. The people

**Asher (about 20).** Not good, not wicked. He is struggling.
- The son of his father's old age, in the purple-hemmed tunic his father gave him.
- Quick, funny, warm with Miriam, Dan and his friends; brilliant with animals (he trained the dog to do his work, and teaches Dan the whistle).
- He lives at night (the village fire, travellers' talk of the lit cities beyond the Jordan) and the farm lives at dawn. That is why he can't get up, and why the flock suffers.
- He teases Amos, looks up to him, and resents that Amos fits the house he can't.
- He bargains with every rule instead of breaking it outright. In the far country, nobody asks him to keep any of them.

His sins in the far country are the ones that make sense: the false god (he pours the first cup to Tyche, the city's Fortune, whose name is inside his new one: the opposite of "God's part first"), the married woman (Jes), and dishonouring his father, which is already in the demand. And the boast that ruins him: "I have no father now. No brother."

**His name.** Asher means *happy, blessed* (Gen 30:13). Kallias renames him Eutychos, *lucky*, and he takes it, swapping the blessing his father gave him for luck he pays for. In Scene 9 the name goes to the next boy for free. At home, the first word his father says is "Asher."

**Eliab (about 65): confident, articulate, in command.** He is God in the parable, so his love is active:
- He built the farm out of the lean years with his firstborn beside him, and he still runs it: he knows every animal ("One short." "The ewe with the torn ear.").
- He goes out into the dark himself after one lost ewe and comes back with her on his shoulders (Luke 15:5).
- He gives orders crisply and explains his rules with reasons.
- He gives Asher grace upon grace (2a), and takes nothing from Amos. He admits the lean years plainly: "When thou wert small, there was no one else to wake. There is now."
- At the village gate he won't let anyone push his son, not even to save his own vines.
- He answers his son at the table, plainly, names the cost, and then grants it, because the choice is Asher's.
- His household does not always agree. On the night of the division Micah holds back the wax: "Master, he will sell your father's vines." Eliab: "Then they are his to sell." He stays right, but he is not safe.
- He keeps the calf fattened without telling anyone why, and he watches the road every dusk.
- He never forbids, never drags anyone, never bars the gate.
- **Once, alone at the lamp** after Asher has gone, he weeps without a word. Then he wipes his face and goes back to give the evening orders. That is the only time we see him break (your rule: weeping, never a lament).

In v2, 8 of his lines were "I will not…", and his answer to the demand was silence and "He asked." All of that is gone.

**The household and the village:**
- **Micah:** old steward. Keeps the count and the keys to the store, and remembers the lean years.
- **Miriam:** runs the house and the ovens. She raised Asher: "I have woken thee every morning since thy mother died. I am not tired of it yet. Up."
- **Dan:** the boy who helps Asher with the flock and does more of the work. Speaks the servant's line in Luke 15:27.
- **Asher's dog:** does Asher's work. Stays home when he leaves and lies at the gate each dusk with Eliab. On the return, it runs at the father's heels.
- **Shallum (new):** foreman of the hired men. Runs the threshing floor and the red calf.
- **The hired men** (five or six), fed at dawn from Eliab's own hand: the picture Asher remembers in the pig fields. Amos works among them as one of them.
- **Nahum:** Eliab's friend of thirty years, who trod those vines beside him. At the gate he cheats out of loyalty, not greed: a light stone and an insulting price, so the boy is shamed into walking away and the vines stay in Eliab's family. At the feast he eats without a word.
- **The elders:** two or three on the bench at the village gate, silent witnesses to the sale (Ruth 4).
- **The traveller:** sells Asher the lit cities at the village fire (Scene 2); months later, at the city's west gate, offers him the road home and carries back his answer, "The lamps here burn all night" (Scene 7); brings it, and the rumour, to the farm gate (Scene 8).
- **Joel:** neighbour. Invites Amos to the wedding.

**Amos (about 40).** The firstborn of the lean years. He threshed beside his father before there were hired men, and he never stopped working like one. He asked his father for something once, a kid for his friends when he was ten, and heard "We had four goats, and a winter coming". He never asked again: not for a kid, not for a wife, not for a feast. For twenty years he has watched the son of his father's old age receive everything. His love for Asher is old protectiveness that thins into duty and dies in Scene 8 (2a). At supper: "Write mine if thou must, Father. Then keep it. I am not going anywhere." Luke 15:31 answers him: "all that I have is thine." He owned everything the whole time, and never took it.

His one lie, "I sent the dog out with them", is told through his teeth for his father's sake, and his father lets it stand. So when Amos says "neither transgressed I at any time thy commandment", we know of one transgression, forgiven before he knew it. He mirrors Jotham and Eleazar: a righteous man who quotes Scripture to justify his grievance ("the elder shall serve the younger").

**Kallias.** He counts who stands behind the boy. "Thy father must own half the hills." "And thy brother, would he come for thee?" He sees the purple hem: "Like a lord's son. A lord's son never counts." Each answer goes on his tablet, along with every coin Asher spends, measured against the sum he arrived with. Kallias knows the night the money ends before Asher does, and draws a stroke under it. In Scene 9 Asher, hiding in the street, watches him at the new boy's ear.

**Philon.** His name means *friend*. The loudest friend and the cheapest: "Buy me twice, Eutychos! I am the cheapest friend at this table." Asher, drunk and happy, throws an arm round his neck: "Thou art more brother to me than my brother ever was." Philon: "Then buy thy brother another cup!" Asher pays his tab to Kallias. He is the brother Asher swaps Amos for, and in Scene 9 it is Philon's voice that calls "Eutychos!" to the new boy. When the coin falls and Asher asks him for one, Philon gives him his own words back: "Ask your father. He gives to any man who asks."

**Jes.** Subtle while he has silver: she chooses him and says very little. She is the far country's answer to Amos. Where his brother said "thou wishest him dead" with grief, she says the same fact as praise: "Every man in this court is waiting for his father to die. Thou alone had the courage to say it to his face." That is evil called good (Isa 5:20), using "courage", the word his father gave him at the gate. It works because nobody at home ever praised him. Her other line is "Here, no one wakes thee." She gives no orders, no dares, no "See? Nothing fell." When the silver is gone, she is beside the new boy, without a word, and in the street she is the only one who sees Asher; she looks away.

**The frame (your mapping, Luke 15:1–3).**
- The father is God.
- **Hezron**, the publican at Jesus's knee, is the younger son. One line at the gate gives his story: "His own father will not say his name." That's why the father who runs breaks him. At the end he lifts the lead tax seal from his neck and lays it on the ground: his living, given up.
- **Tirzah** is his young daughter. When he weeps, she calls him "Abba".
- **The shepherd boy** (17, plate approved 17 Oct, replacing v2's old man) sits near Jesus with his staff, and a sling at his rope belt. When Jesus asks "What man of you, having an hundred sheep…", it is the boy who answers: "He doth. Every shepherd doth." He is a young shepherd with a sling, like Asher, and he is sure every shepherd goes after the lost one. In Scene 2, Asher doesn't; his father does. The boy is there again in Scenes 11 and 16.
- **Jotham and Eleazar**, the Pharisees at the gate, are the elder brother. To them Hezron is a traitor who took Rome's silver from his own people, a brother in Abraham they have disowned. Jotham softens over the four hillside scenes (at the law in Scene 4A his stylus touches the wax and stops; in Scene 11 he has written nothing, and smooths the old lines away). At the end he sets his tablet, his record of accusations, down on the wall and steps through. Eleazar hardens: in Scene 4A he quotes the law of the rebellious son (Deut 21:18-21), "all the men of his city shall stone him with stones", and his hand finds a stone on the wall. At the end it is still there, while Jesus holds bread out toward him.

## 6. Dialogue rules for the rewrite

1. **Every line must do at least one of these, or it's cut:**
   - move the story (the portion, the silver, the road, the return);
   - press a rule against Asher's will;
   - reveal someone under pressure;
   - plant something that pays off later.
2. **Eliab:** he states things and gives orders, with reasons. No "I will not…" lines. He never forbids and never bars a door. His favour to Asher is always grace: he gives what is needed and takes nothing from Amos.
3. **Asher:** humour is his armour. His honesty breaks through it once a scene at most.
4. **Amos:** short, plain, held back. He never says "brother" after Scene 8.
5. **The Tolkien voice everywhere** (owner, 19 Oct): the solemn cadence and inverted word order of *The Return of the King*, plain strong words, no thee or thou, no contractions. Scripture is rendered in the same voice, faithful in meaning; the approved renderings are fixed in `check_v3_screenplay.py`. Kallias speaks like Wormtongue, honeyed questions dressed as compliments. **Registers (20 Oct):** high for Eliab, Scripture, the hillside, Kallias and the elders; plainer for Asher, Dan, Philon, the traveller, Miriam and the hired men, as Tolkien's hobbits speak plainer than Gandalf. Inversion only where a line carries weight, at most once a scene.
6. **Free-will check on every scene:** nobody is stopped, forced or locked in. Every door that matters stays visibly open.
7. **Written for H3 speech:** one speaker per window, and every line short enough to sit whole inside one window (about 14 seconds). Eliab's answer at the table and the long KJV speeches are cut into short sentences across shots. Nobody talks over anyone.
